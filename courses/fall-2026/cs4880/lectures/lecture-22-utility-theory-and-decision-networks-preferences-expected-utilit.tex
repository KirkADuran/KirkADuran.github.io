\section{From Knowing to Choosing}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{frame}[t]{Last Time: The Robot Estimated Its State}
  \small
  Dynamic Bayesian networks and filters gave the robot a belief about the current world:
  \[
    bel_t(x)=P(X_t=x\mid z_{1:t}).
  \]

  \begin{itemize}
    \item HMMs represented compact discrete hidden state.
    \item Kalman filters represented linear-Gaussian continuous state.
    \item Particle filters represented flexible beliefs using weighted samples.
  \end{itemize}


  \equationlegend{$bel_t(x)$: belief assigned to candidate state $x$ at time $t$; $X_t$: hidden state; $z_{1:t}$: observations through $t$; $P(\cdot\mid\cdot)$: probability given evidence. Known control history is suppressed.}

  \begin{keyidea}
    State estimation gives the agent beliefs about the world; decision theory uses those beliefs to choose actions.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

  \small
  A planner proposes options; the robot must choose which option to execute under uncertainty.
  \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      The choice depends on:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item current position belief;
        \item obstacles and pedestrian traffic;
        \item remaining time and battery;
        \item delivery priority.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \lectureimage[1.8in]{slide3.png}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \begin{keyidea}
    Estimate the situation, choose an action, observe the result, and repeat.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{A Good State Estimate Still Does Not Tell Us What to Do}
  \small
  Let $B$ describe whether the shortcut is blocked:
  \[
    P(B=1)=0.35,\qquad P(B=0)=0.65.
  \]
  Throughout the example, write these as $P(\text{Blocked})$ and $P(\text{Clear})$.
  That belief does not answer:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Is the shortcut worth attempting?
    \item Should the robot take a longer detour?
    \item Is another scan worth its time and energy?
  \end{itemize}
  \equationlegend{$B$: blockage variable; $1$: blocked, $0$: clear. $P$: probability given the current evidence, which is suppressed here.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    Probability describes what may happen. Choosing an action also requires preferences over the consequences.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

\section{Utility Theory}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 8
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Utility Turns Preferences into a Numerical Model}
  \small
  A \textbf{utility function} assigns a value to each outcome:
  \[
    U:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.
  \]
  For example,
  \[
    U(\text{fast safe delivery})>U(\text{safe detour})>U(\text{blocked attempt}).
  \]
  For risky choices, the numerical gaps must also encode willingness to trade a chance of a good outcome against a chance of a bad one.
  \equationlegend{$\mathcal{O}$: set of outcomes; $o$: one outcome; $U(o)$: its utility; $\mathbb{R}$: real numbers; $>$: preferred to.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    Ranking certain outcomes is only the start. Expected utility needs a scale that also represents preferences over uncertain outcomes.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}


% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 9
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Under Uncertainty, Use Expected Utility}
  \small
  Average the utility of each possible outcome, weighted by its probability:
  \[
    EU(a\mid e)=\sum_o P(o\mid a,e)U(o).
  \]
  Then choose among feasible actions:
  \[
    a^*=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}} EU(a\mid e).
  \]
  \equationlegend{$a$: candidate action; $e$: evidence already observed; $o$: possible outcome; $P(o\mid a,e)$: outcome probability under the chosen action and evidence. $\sum_o$: sum over outcomes; $\mathcal{A}$: feasible action set; $\arg\max$: return a maximizing action; $a^*$: chosen action.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    Probability supplies the weights; utility supplies the values. We will suppress fixed evidence $e$ in the numerical example.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 10
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Worked Decision: Shortcut or Detour?}
  \small
  Current belief: $P(\text{Clear})=0.65$, $P(\text{Blocked})=0.35$.
  \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
    \begin{tabular}{l|rr}
      \textbf{Utility $U(a,b)$} & \textbf{Clear ($b=0$)} & \textbf{Blocked ($b=1$)}\\\hline
      Shortcut & $80$ & $-60$\\
      Detour & $45$ & $45$\\
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  These are illustrative preference values, not measured travel times. The detour is unaffected by shortcut blockage in this model.
  \equationlegend{$a$: route action; $b$: value of blockage variable $B$; $U(a,b)$: utility of the resulting route-segment outcome. Higher is preferred; negative values indicate outcomes worse than the chosen zero.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    Compare both actions under the same belief and the same utility scale.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 11
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Expected Utility of the Shortcut}
  \small
  \[
    EU(\text{Shortcut})
      =0.65(80)+0.35(-60)
  \]
  \[
      =52-21
      =31.
  \]

  The shortcut is more likely to be clear than blocked, but the blocked outcome is costly enough to reduce its expected utility substantially.

  \equationlegend{$EU(\text{Shortcut})$: expected utility of the shortcut; $0.65,0.35$: clear/blocked probabilities; $80,-60$: their utilities. Each product is probability $\times$ utility; $31$: their sum.}

  \begin{keyidea}
    Expected utility combines the upside and downside of an action in one probability-weighted quantity.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 12
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Expected Utility of the Detour}
  \small
  For the simplified example, the detour is predictable:
  \[
    EU(\text{Detour})=45.
  \]

  Therefore
  \[
    45>31.
  \]

  The rational one-step choice is the detour.

  \equationlegend{$EU(\text{Detour})$: expected utility of the detour, $45$ in both world states. $31$: shortcut value; $45>31$: detour has greater expected utility under the same belief.}

  \begin{keyidea}
    Rational choice is about expected preference, not simply choosing the most probable outcome.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 13
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{When Would We Switch to the Shortcut?}
  \small
  Let $p$ be the probability that the shortcut is clear. It beats the detour when
  \[
    \underbrace{80p-60(1-p)}_{EU(\text{Shortcut})}>\underbrace{45}_{EU(\text{Detour})}.
  \]
  Rearranging gives
  \[
    140p>105\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p>0.75.
  \]
  At $p=0.75$ the actions tie; at our current $p=0.65$, choose the detour.
  \equationlegend{$p=P(\text{Clear})$; $1-p=P(\text{Blocked})$; $80,-60,45$: utilities from the table. $EU$: expected utility; $>$: strict preference here; $\Longrightarrow$: implies.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    ``More likely clear than blocked'' is insufficient. These utilities require more than a 75\% chance of a clear shortcut.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 14
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Changing the Utility Scale Preserves the Decision}
  \small
  Under the standard expected-utility consistency assumptions on preferences over lotteries, utilities represent choices under uncertainty.
  \[
    U'(o)=\alpha U(o)+\beta,\qquad \alpha>0.
  \]
  Then
  \[
    EU'(a)=\alpha EU(a)+\beta.
  \]
  A positive scaling and a common shift preserve which action has the greatest expected utility.
  \equationlegend{$o$: outcome; $a$: action; $U,U'$: original/transformed utilities; $EU,EU'$: corresponding expected utilities. $\alpha$: positive scale; $\beta$: common offset. Outcome probabilities sum to $1$. A lottery is a probability distribution over outcomes.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    Arbitrary increasing transformations preserve rankings of certain outcomes, but can change decisions under uncertainty. Utility gaps matter.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 15
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{A Robot Usually Has More Than One Objective}
  \small
  The robot may simultaneously care about:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item travel time;
    \item energy consumption;
    \item collision and pedestrian risk;
    \item progress toward completing the delivery;
    \item reliability and schedule slack.
  \end{itemize}

  These objectives can conflict.

  \begin{keyidea}
    Real decision systems often optimize several competing attributes, so the utility model must make those tradeoffs explicit.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 16
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{One Engineering Approximation: Multiattribute Utility}
  \small
  Combine attribute scores on stated, comparable scales:
  \[
    U(o)=w_tu_t(o)+w_eu_e(o)+w_su_s(o)+w_pu_p(o).
  \]
  \equationlegend{$o$: outcome; $u_t,u_e,u_s,u_p$: scores for time, energy, safety, and progress, all oriented so higher is better. $w_t,w_e,w_s,w_p$: nonnegative tradeoff weights; here subscripts identify attributes, not time steps.}
  A larger weight matters relative to the score's scale: changing a score's units requires adjusting its weight.
  \begin{keyidea}
    An additive model assumes tradeoffs can be separated this way. Attribute interactions may require a richer utility model.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 17
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Changing the Weights Can Change the Robot's Behavior}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Time-heavy}
        Large penalty for delay. The robot favors feasible routes with smaller expected delay.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Energy-heavy}
        Battery is scarce. The robot avoids steep ramps, long scans, and unnecessary detours.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Safety-heavy}
        The robot prefers conservative routes through uncertain pedestrian traffic.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \begin{keyidea}
    The same probability model can produce very different behavior under different utility functions.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 18
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Not Every Requirement Should Be ``Just Another Weight''}
  \small
  Weighted utilities make tradeoffs possible by design.

  But some requirements may instead be treated as \textbf{hard constraints}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item exclude actions that violate safety envelopes;
    \item enforce legal or operational limits;
    \item optimize utility only among feasible actions.
  \end{itemize}

  This matters when a tiny probability of a catastrophic outcome should not be casually exchanged for a small improvement in speed.

  \begin{keyidea}
    Utility maximization is only as sensible as the objectives, constraints, and action set supplied to it.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

\section{Decision Networks}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 19
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Decision Networks: Three Node Types}
  \small
  A \textbf{decision network} or \textbf{influence diagram} contains three node types:

  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Chance node}
        Uncertain random variable.

        Example: shortcut blocked?
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Decision node}
        Choice controlled by the agent.

        Example: shortcut, detour, scan, wait.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Utility node}
        Preference over resulting outcomes.

        Example: time + energy + safety + progress.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \begin{keyidea}
    A decision network combines uncertainty, controllable choices, and utility in one graphical model.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 20
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Robot Decision Network: One Route Choice}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,link/.style={-{Stealth[length=2mm]},thick}]
      \node[draw,ellipse,minimum width=2.3cm] (b) at (0,1.0) {Blocked? $B$};
      \node[draw,rectangle,minimum width=2.3cm] (a) at (0,-0.7) {Route $A$};
      \node[draw,diamond,aspect=2.2,minimum width=2.5cm] (u) at (5,0.15) {Utility $U$};
      \draw[link] (b)--(u); \draw[link] (a)--(u);
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  The utility table depends on the chosen route and the hidden blockage state. There is no arrow from $B$ into $A$: blockage is not directly observed.
  \equationlegend{$B$: chance variable (ellipse); $A$: controlled choice (rectangle); $U(a,b)$: utility function (diamond), not a probability table. Arrows into $U$ identify its inputs.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    Choose a route using a belief about blockage, without pretending the hidden state is known.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% Slide 21
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Information Arcs Mean ``Known Before the Decision''}
  \small
  Suppose a scan reading is available before the route is chosen.
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,link/.style={-{Stealth[length=2mm]},thick}]
      \node[draw,ellipse] (b) at (0,0) {Blocked? $B$};

  \begin{keyidea}
    A decision node receives information, not a CPT. The reading is known; the blockage state remains uncertain.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 22
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Evaluate Using Only Information Available at Decision Time}
  \small
  After a reading $z$, average over the still-hidden blockage state:
  \[
    EU(a\mid z)=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}P(B=b\mid Z=z)U(a,b).
  \]
  Then select a route for that reading:
  \[
    a^*(z)=\arg\max_{a\in\{\text{Shortcut},\text{Detour}\}} EU(a\mid z).
  \]
  \equationlegend{$b$: possible blockage value; $z$: observed reading; $P(B=b\mid Z=z)$: posterior belief. $U(a,b)$: utility table; $a^*(z)$: decision rule mapping a reading to a route. Fixed earlier evidence is suppressed.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    Average over hidden states before maximizing. Maximizing separately for each hidden state would incorrectly give the robot perfect information.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 23
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{The Belief State Plugs Directly Into the Decision}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,
      every node/.style={draw,rounded corners,align=center,minimum width=2.7cm,minimum height=1.0cm,font=\small}]
      \node (f) at (0,0) {Filter\\Current belief $b_t$};
      \node (d) at (4,0) {Decision model\\Expected utilities};
      \node (a) at (8,0) {Next action\\Maximize utility};
      \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]},thick] (f)--(d);
      \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]},thick] (d)--(a);
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  The filter updates what the robot believes. The decision model uses that distribution to compare consequences of feasible actions.
  \equationlegend{$b_t$: current belief distribution at time $t$. In the worked example it supplies the probabilities of clear and blocked.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    State estimation and action selection are complementary steps in the same controller.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 24
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{One-Step Decision Procedure}
  \small
  At each control cycle:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item update the belief state $b_t$;
    \item generate the feasible action set $\mathcal{A}(b_t)$;
    \item predict possible outcomes for each action;
    \item compute expected utility for each action;
    \item execute a maximizing feasible action.
  \end{enumerate}

  \equationlegend{$b_t$: current belief at time $t$; $\mathcal{A}(b_t)$: actions allowed under that belief and the operational constraints; expected utility averages their outcome utilities.}

  \begin{keyidea}
    For movement, this compares one segment at a time. A scan needs a short lookahead: scan, observe, then choose a route.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

\section{Value of Information}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 25
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Value the Plan: Scan, Observe, Then Choose}
  \small
  Compare the best route now with the best route after each possible reading:
  \[
    V_{\text{now}}=\max_a EU(a\mid e),\qquad
    V_{\text{with}}=\sum_z P(z\mid e)\max_a EU(a\mid e,z).
  \]
  The \textbf{gross value of information} is $V_{\text{with}}-V_{\text{now}}$.
  Acquire the reading when this improvement exceeds its acquisition cost.
  \equationlegend{$e$: current evidence; $a$: route choice; $z$: possible reading; $EU$: expected utility. $V_{\text{now}}$: optimal value before sensing; $V_{\text{with}}$: expected optimal value after sensing, before its cost. Sum over readings; choose the route after each reading.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    A scan's immediate cost buys a later decision. Evaluating only its immediate reward would miss its benefit.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 26
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Will a Better Measurement Change the Decision?}
  \small
  The prior probability of blockage is $P(\text{Blocked})=0.35$.
  Without scanning, choose the detour: $V_{\text{now}}=45$.

  A scan costs $c=5$ utility units and has the sensor model
  \[
    P(+\mid\text{Blocked})=0.90,\qquad P(+\mid\text{Clear})=0.10.
  \]
  \equationlegend{$+$: sensor reports blockage; $-$: sensor reports clear. These are readings, not true states. $c$: fixed additive utility cost; $V_{\text{now}}$: value of the best route without another reading.}
  Assume the world stays fixed during this brief scan, both routes remain available, and its delay and energy cost are captured by $c$.
  \begin{keyidea}
    We are valuing one imperfect measurement before one route choice, using the same utilities as before.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 27
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{The Scan Produces Two Possible Belief Updates}
  \small
  Weight each sensor likelihood by its prior state probability:
  \[
    P(+)=0.90(0.35)+0.10(0.65)=0.38,\qquad P(-)=1-0.38=0.62.
  \]
  Bayes' rule gives
  \[
    P(\text{Blocked}\mid+)=\frac{0.90(0.35)}{0.38}\approx0.829,
  \]
  \[
    P(\text{Blocked}\mid-)=\frac{0.10(0.35)}{0.62}\approx0.0565.
  \]
  \equationlegend{$P(+),P(-)$: reading probabilities; $P(\text{Blocked}\mid+)$ and $P(\text{Blocked}\mid-)$: posterior blockage beliefs. The negative-reading likelihood under blockage is $1-0.90=0.10$; denominators normalize each posterior.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    Predict how often each reading occurs, then update the belief separately for each branch.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Slide 28
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{After the Scan, Choose a Route for Each Reading}
  \small
  For a \textbf{positive} reading, blockage probability is about $0.829$:
  \[
    EU(\text{Shortcut}\mid+)\approx(1-0.829)(80)+0.829(-60)\approx-36.1.
  \]
  Choose the \textbf{detour}, whose utility is $45$.

  \medskip
  For a \textbf{negative} reading, blockage probability is about $0.0565$:
  \[
    EU(\text{Shortcut}\mid-)\approx(1-0.0565)(80)+0.0565(-60)\approx72.1.
  \]
  Choose the \textbf{shortcut}, since $72.1>45$.
  \equationlegend{$EU(a\mid+)$ and $EU(a\mid-)$: route values after the reading, before sensing cost. $80,-60$: shortcut utilities; $45$: detour utility. Displayed posteriors are rounded; calculations use full precision.}
  \begin{keyidea}
    The conditional plan is: positive $\rightarrow$ detour; negative $\rightarrow$ shortcut.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{Information Can Be Accurate and Still Have Zero Decision Value}

  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]

  Repeating this loop does not by itself add long-term planning.
  \begin{keyidea}

  \end{keyidea}

% Slide 32

\begin{frame}[t]{This Gives Us a Myopic Decision Controller}
  \small
  \begin{block}{One-step controller}
    \ttfamily\small
    while delivery not complete:\\
    \quad b\_t = update\_belief(sensor\_data)\\
    \quad actions = feasible\_actions(b\_t)\\
    \quad for a in actions:\\
    \qquad EU[a] = expected\_immediate\_utility(a,b\_t)\\
    \quad execute(argmax\_a EU[a])
  \end{block}

  Here \texttt{b\_t} is the current belief, \texttt{a} a feasible action, and \texttt{EU[a]} its expected immediate utility.

  This version is \textbf{myopic}: it omits consequences beyond the immediate segment. To include sensing, evaluate the scan--then--route plan from the previous example, rather than just the scan cost.

  \begin{keyidea}
    Repeated one-step optimization is a useful reactive strategy, but repeated local optimality does not guarantee globally good behavior.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

% Slide 33

\begin{frame}[t]{Failure Mode: Locally Rational Actions Can Oscillate}
  \small
  Imagine two similar corridors whose estimated congestion changes slightly with each noisy scan.

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item At time $t$, left has slightly higher immediate expected utility $\rightarrow$ turn left.
    \item At time $t+1$, a new reading makes right slightly better $\rightarrow$ turn right.
    \item At time $t+2$, noise flips the preference again $\rightarrow$ turn left.
  \end{enumerate}

  \[
    \text{Left} \leftrightarrow \text{Right} \leftrightarrow \text{Left} \leftrightarrow \cdots
  \]

  \equationlegend{$t,t+1,t+2$: successive control cycles; $\rightarrow$: controller chooses that direction; $\leftrightarrow$: repeated switching, not a probabilistic dependency.}

  \begin{keyidea}
    A controller that only asks ``what is best this instant?'' can thrash when actions have switching costs or delayed effects.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{The Question Has Changed Again}
  \small
  So far the robot chooses
  \[
    a_t^*=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}(b_t)} EU(a\mid b_t).
  \]

  But repeated autonomous control asks something stronger:

  \begin{center}
    \Large\textbf{What action is best when I also care about the future states and future decisions it creates?}
  \end{center}

  \equationlegend{$t$: current time; $b_t$: current belief; $\mathcal{A}(b_t)$: feasible actions; $EU(a\mid b_t)$: utility expected under that belief for the current outcome horizon; $a_t^*$: maximizing action.}

  \begin{keyidea}
    Once actions influence future opportunities, rational decision making must reason across sequences of states and actions.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{Next: Markov Decision Processes}
  \small
  Lecture 23 will first assume the state is fully observed and formalize:

  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{states}: what situation is the robot in?
    \item \textbf{actions}: what can the robot do?
    \item \textbf{transitions}: where might each action take it?
    \item \textbf{rewards}: what immediate signal does a transition produce?
    \item \textbf{policy}: what should the robot do in every state?
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{keyidea}
    Our one-decision examples become sequential planning problems. MDPs use observed states; partially observable MDPs (POMDPs) use hidden states and beliefs. Decision networks can also represent multiple decisions.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{Takeaways}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Probability describes uncertain outcomes; utility describes preferences over those outcomes.
    \item Expected utility combines probability and preference: choose the action with maximum expected utility.
    \item Multiattribute utilities make tradeoffs explicit, but weights and modeling assumptions directly shape behavior.
    \item Decision networks combine chance variables, decision variables, information, and utility.
    \item Value of information asks whether better evidence is worth its sensing cost because it changes downstream decisions.
    \item Repeating immediate-utility choices gives a myopic controller; modeling future choices requires an explicit planning horizon.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{keyidea}
    The robot can now decide what to do next. Lecture 23 asks how it should decide when ``next'' changes everything that comes after.
  \end{keyidea}
\end{frame}
